Day 3 lab: read a token and an NFT

The course chain for day 3 holds a dollar token, cUSD, built the way the big stablecoins are: an issuer who creates new money, a blacklist only the issuer controls, and code that sits behind a proxy. It also holds an NFT, the Course Badge, and a shop that sells badges for ETH. Seventeen blocks of history were built for this lab. You read them in Otterscan, the open-source explorer you used on day 1 [1]. Then you mint a badge of your own and read it back.

Write your answers down as short notes. Your instructor checks them with you.

If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages. They show the same history, so every question below has the same answer there. For part 2, the saved pages hold a recorded run of the mint page.

Where to start

  • Your instructor tells you where to start: the worked example, or straight at the problem.
  • You may always do the worked example first.
  • After the problem, part 2: you mint a badge and read it in the explorer.
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

The questions you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: a hash, an amount, a log. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [11]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction.

  1. In block 4, Account 1 sends cUSD. Who receives it, and how much? Why is the receiver not in the transaction's To field?
  2. Block 4's Transfer log records the amount as 2500000000. Why is that 2,500 cUSD and not 2.5 billion? Where on the chain does the explorer find out?
  3. People send their cUSD transactions to 0xe7f1…0512. Where is the code that runs when they do? Who can swap that code for different code, and what would that change? Give your confidence and the evidence for it.
  4. Who can freeze an address's cUSD? Which block froze one, and which address?
  5. Block 7 holds a transaction that failed. What did it try to do, why did it fail, and what did it still cost its sender?
  6. In block 8, Account 1 sends cUSD to Account 3, which is frozen. Did it work? What does that tell you about what the freeze stops?
  7. Add up Account 3's cUSD from the Transfer logs alone. What does the contract say Account 3 holds at block 17? Explain the difference. How confident are you, and what would a tool that only reads Transfer logs report about Account 3?
  8. Block 11 creates badge 1. Who created it, who first held it, and how can you tell from the log that it is an NFT and not a cUSD transfer?
  9. Where is badge 1's name and picture kept? Is the name on the badge evidence of who made it?
  10. Block 15: who paid whom, how much, and who got badge 1? Account 4 received ETH but sent no transaction in that block: how did the ETH arrive? Give your confidence.

Worked example: new money in block 3

This walks through one token call, field by field. Question 1 onward use the same pages.

Find it

  1. Open the explorer.
  2. Type 3 in the search box and press Enter. You are on block 3's page.
  3. Note the block's timestamp: 16 Sep 2026, 14:00:36 UTC.
  4. Open the block's one transaction. Its hash is 0x8a26810f5f5f0c4701c72507945289bafca405635ed7bec1c93cea737d9b39a5.

  • A token is a contract. The ETH value is 0: no ETH moved. cUSD is not a coin the chain itself keeps. It is a contract that keeps its own list of balances, and a transaction changes that list by calling one of the contract's functions [8] [10].
  • The input names the call. Its first 4 bytes, 0x40c10f19, are mint(address,uint256) in the table above. The next 32 bytes are the address, Account 1, padded with zeros. The last 32 bytes are the amount in hexadecimal: 0x2540be400 is 10,000,000,000.
  • Decimals. A token stores whole numbers only. Its decimals says where the decimal point goes [8]. cUSD's is 6, so 10,000,000,000 is 10,000.000000 cUSD. The explorer asks the contract for this number and shows "10,000 cUSD".
  • The log is the record. Open the Logs tab. The contract wrote one Transfer log: from 0x0000…0000 to Account 1, 10,000 cUSD. A contract that creates tokens records it as a transfer from the zero address [8], so this is new money, not a payment from someone. The log was written by 0xe7f1…0512, the cUSD address.
  • Proxy. Open the Trace tab. It has two lines: a call to Course Dollar (cUSD), then a delegatecall to Course Dollar (cUSD) again. Click each name: the first is 0xe7f1…0512, the second 0x5FbDB2315678afecb367f032d93F642f64180aa3. That is a proxy at work: the address people pay holds the balances, and it borrows another contract's code to change them [4] [5]. Both show the same name because the explorer asks each contract what it is called, and the code contract gives the name written in its code. A name on an explorer page is the contract's own claim; the address is what identifies it. Question 3 asks you to follow the proxy further.

A statement from this page: "Account 0 created 10,000 new cUSD and credited it to Account 1 on 16 Sep 2026 at 14:00:36 UTC." The call and the log are facts the chain records. Calling Account 0 "the issuer" is a judgment: the contract's code and its owner are the evidence for it (question 4).

The problem

Answer the questions above. These hints say where to look.

  1. Who received. Open block 4's transaction. Read "Tokens Transferred" and the Logs tab as well as "Interacted With (To)". The To field is the contract called; the receiver is inside the call and the log [8].
  2. Decimals. Search for the cUSD address and open its Contract tab: the code declares decimals. You can also check the arithmetic by hand, as in the worked example.
  3. Where the code is. Open block 2's transaction, which created the cUSD address, and read its log: Upgraded names the address of the code. A standard proxy keeps that address in a fixed storage slot and announces each change with an Upgraded log, so explorers know where to look [5]. This explorer does not show the slot on the cUSD address's page; some explorers do. Compare the Upgraded log with the delegatecall in block 3's Trace tab. Then read upgradeTo on the Contract tab: who may call it? On the saved pages, "Contract reads at block 17" gives the slot.
  4. Freezing. Read addBlackList on the cUSD Contract tab, then find the block whose transaction calls it (selector 0x0ecb93c0) and read its AddedBlackList log. USDT, a dollar stablecoin, has the same functions: only its owner can freeze an address or destroy a frozen address's balance [3].
  5. A failed transaction. Open block 7's transaction and read its Status line, which gives the error. A failed transaction still pays for the gas it used [6].
  6. Sending to a frozen address. Open block 8's transaction and its Transfer log. Then read transfer on the Contract tab: whose address does it check? USDT's checks the same one [3].
  7. Account 3's balance. Search for Account 3's address: its page lists only block 7, the transaction it sent. The explorer lists the transactions an address sent or was sent, not the token logs that name it. Open the cUSD address's page instead, which lists every call to cUSD, and read the Transfer logs of blocks 5 and 8: both pay Account 3, and nothing goes out. Then open block 9's transaction: its log is DestroyedBlackFunds, and there is no Transfer log. On the mint page, under "Ask the contracts", ask balanceOf for Account 3. USDT's destroyBlackFunds writes no Transfer log either [3].
  8. The badge. Open block 11's transaction and its Logs tab. A badge's Transfer log has the same name as a cUSD Transfer, but carries the badge number as an indexed topic and no amount [2]. Compare the number of topics with block 4's log. A badge created out of nothing is a Transfer from the zero address [2].
  9. Metadata. Block 11's input data holds a long text that starts data:application/json,. That is the badge's metadata: a name, a description and an image, in the shape the NFT standard describes [2]. On the mint page, ask tokenURI for badge 1 to read what the contract stored. Many real NFTs store only a link here: the metadata and the picture sit outside the chain, often on a web server or on IPFS, a shared file network [12].
  10. Who paid whom. Open block 15's transaction. Account 2 sent ETH to the shop. Read the Logs tab for the badge, and look under "Interacted With (To)" for the TRANSFER lines: the explorer finds them by asking the node to replay the transaction [7]. The explorer's first TRANSFER line is the transaction's own 0.5 ETH; the lines after it are what the shop passed on. The saved pages list only those.

Part 2: mint a badge

You need the live chain and a seat number from your instructor. If the chain is down, answer from the recorded run instead.

  1. Open the mint page and load your seat.
  2. Give your badge a name and a description, and build the mint. Read the call the page shows you before you send it.
  3. Sign it and send it. The page waits for the next block, then reads your badge back from the contract.
  4. Open your transaction in the explorer from the link the page gives you.
  5. Read the panel at the bottom of the mint page: it lists every question the page asked the node [9].

Answer:

  1. Your transaction's hash, its block, and your badge's number. Where did the page find the number?
  2. In the explorer, read your transaction's Transfer log. Who is it from, who is it to, and how does that match what you see for badge 1 in block 11?
  3. Anyone can call mint with any name and picture. If a badge named "Issued by the instructor" turned up, what would the chain prove about who made it, and what would it not?

Challenge

Optional.

  1. cUSD's total supply was 10,000 after block 3. Read the supply at block 17 under "Contract reads at block 17" on the saved pages, and account for the difference using only what the chain records. Which part would you miss with Transfer logs alone?
  2. Suppose the issuer swaps cUSD's code tomorrow. Which log would show it, which address would send the transaction, and why would every cUSD holder's balance still be there afterwards?
