Day 6 lab: follow the ransom to the first service

The course runs a small Bitcoin chain of its own, built for this lab, with the same address formats and transaction rules as Bitcoin. On it, two ransomware victims have paid a ransom (the incident is invented). You follow the money in mempool, an open-source block explorer [1], until it reaches the first service, and write an annotated trace: one row per judgment, each with its heuristic, what could make it wrong, and a confidence.

Write your trace as short notes. Your instructor checks it with you.

This chain is not Bitcoin's. The names and amounts are made up (a course faucet pays every payer and customer: walking back, a trail ends at the first coin the faucet paid, and where the faucet's own coins came from is not a question here), and nothing here points to a real person or service. If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages: they show the same transactions, so every question has the same answer there.

Where to start

  • Your instructor tells you where to start: the worked example, or straight at the problem.
  • You may always do the worked example first. It shows you the explorer and two rows of a trace.
  • Then the questions: part 1 (questions 1 to 4), part 2 (questions 5 to 9) and part 3 (questions 10 to 15).
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

Reading this explorer

  • This explorer is offline, so its dollar figures, fee badges and "Accelerate" buttons mean nothing here. Ignore them.
  • The lab's blocks were made seconds apart. Use block numbers, not times, to put events in order.
  • The explorer shows no names, and it does not mark which output is change or a payment. Deciding that is your job.
  • An address page lists the transactions that pay it and spend from it. An output with no spend after it has not been spent yet.
  • On a transaction's page, the "Details" button shows each input's address type and the other fields.
  • Address types are visible in the address: bc1q is P2WPKH, bc1p is Taproot (P2TR) and 3 is P2SH [6] [7] [8].
  • Use a window at least 800 pixels wide. A narrower one shows a different, mobile page.

How you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: an address, an amount, a block. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [2]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction. Never give a percentage.

Worked example: the victims' payments

This follows the ransom into the ransom addresses, two transactions before your trail starts. It shows you the explorer and two finished rows: reading one transaction, then following its change one hop. Question 1 onward use the same pages.

Find it

  1. Open the explorer, paste bc1qa04puwqwujp04pa3x5ldysqmjcmqg06e2vmyf4 into the search box and press Enter. Its page lists two transactions.
  2. Open the older one, in block 15. Its ID is 63a4f3fbf0d6ab2d9071b2bf2c92f73456dac21108b7a8783afc2aee7a00e20a.

Read it

Inputbc1qcfkf9aywjynl9pfsmn8hg2xvu35098fn4vvtag, 50.00000000 BTC, type P2WPKH
Output 0bc1qa04puwqwujp04pa3x5ldysqmjcmqg06e2vmyf4, 1.20000000 BTC
Output 1bc1qrelaavatfxpcukyllg6px7d95cwg0t9m97fh4u, 48.79999154 BTC
Fee846 sats, for a transaction of 141 vB: about 6 sats per vB
  • Outputs, not balances. Bitcoin records outputs, each an amount locked to an address, and a transaction spends whole outputs [3]. The input here was one 50.00000000 BTC output. 1.20000000 BTC went to the ransom address; the rest, less the fee, went back to the payer.
  • The fee. No output records the fee: it is what the inputs hold beyond the outputs, inputs minus outputs leaves 846 sats [3].
  • Which output is the ransom? The ransom output is a round number, 1.2 BTC; the other is not. Both addresses are the same type. One clue, so the judgment is below.

Follow the change one hop

  1. On the payment's page, open the change output, bc1qrelaavatfxpcukyllg6px7d95cwg0t9m97fh4u. Its page shows it spent once, in block 16: transaction 76f2564db9b842dc33792e23127648bfb0955c483b01fbd195e5185d256bf960.
  2. That transaction spends the change alone. It pays 0.81230000 BTC to the second ransom address, bc1q0gym25mj00dwfrzc8ypwk5tplvxza0mvx68yeh, and 47.98768308 BTC to a new address. In this invented case the course faucet paid both ransoms (the note at the top), so one wallet paid both; two real victims would not share one.
  • A peel hop. A small amount is paid away, the peel, and the rest rolls on to a new address, the change, which pays again [4]. The peel here is the rounder amount (0.81230000 against 47.98768308). All three addresses are P2WPKH, so type tells them apart no better than in row 0.
  • What the row rests on. You opened this transaction only because row 0 called its input the change. If row 0 is wrong, this row is wrong too.

Low twice over: the amount is again the only clue, and the row stands on row 0, itself Low. A row cannot be surer than the call it stands on. Your trail does not need these rows. It starts at the ransom addresses, but the rows show the format and the two moves you repeat: read a transaction, then follow its change into the next. Back on the first ransom address's page, the newer transaction spends it together with the second ransom address: that is question 1.

The questions

Part 1: the ransom addresses

The victims paid two ransom addresses. Victim 1 paid bc1qa04puwqwujp04pa3x5ldysqmjcmqg06e2vmyf4. Victim 2 paid bc1q0gym25mj00dwfrzc8ypwk5tplvxza0mvx68yeh. Call the transaction that spends them T1, the next one down the trail T2, and so on.

  1. How much did each ransom address receive, and in which block? Which transaction spends them, and in which block?
  2. What does T1 tell you about the two ransom addresses? Write the row: what you see, the heuristic, what could make it wrong, and your confidence.
  3. T1 has two outputs. Which is the payment and which is the change? Name each clue you used, and whether it points one way or is no help. Give your confidence.
  4. One output of T1 is spent in block 21, together with a second input. What does that spend tell you about the owner of that output? Do you follow it as the ransom's trail? Say why.

Part 2: the peel chain

  1. Follow the change output of T1 into T2. Which output is the peel (the small amount paid away) and which is the change that rolls on? Which clues tell them apart? Give your confidence.
  2. Do the same for T3, the next hop. The change sat in a different output position in T2 and in T3. What does that say about using position as a clue?
  3. T1, T2 and T3 now form a pattern. Name it and its heuristic, say what could make it wrong, and give a confidence. Record each peel as a lead: its amount, its address, its hop number, and what that address does next.
  4. The next hop (T4) has two outputs. Which clues separate them? Write what you can say about the change, and how you decide which output to follow. Give your confidence.
  5. T5 is the last hop of the chain. Which output is the peel and which is the change? Where does the peel go next, and what is the address's next transaction?

Part 3: the first service

  1. Open the transaction that spends the peel from T5; call it T6. How many inputs and outputs does it have? What does the co-spend heuristic tell you about the peel's address and the other inputs? What could make it wrong, and how would you check? Give your confidence.
  2. T6's single output is spent in one transaction, T7. How many inputs and outputs does T7 have? What does its shape suggest about the wallet that holds the funds? Give your confidence, and say why the trace stops here.
  3. Now trace backward, as the lesson's Mt. Gox case does. Start at the peel from T5, where T6 swept it, and walk back input by input: which transaction paid it, what did that transaction spend, and so on, until you reach T4. Does walking back settle which of T4's outputs was the change? Write the row, with your confidence.
  4. A colleague looking at the same sweep says: "There's a second deposit in there with the same moves. Same guy. Add it to the total." Two of T6's twelve inputs are 0.05 BTC deposits, and each is the last peel of a peel chain: round peels on most hops, the change rolled on to a fresh P2WPKH address each hop. One is D, from T5. Is the other ransom money too? Before you open the transaction that paid the other deposit, write down one choice and a confidence (High, Moderate or Low):
    1. yes: same amount, same habits, same service, so the thief paid in twice;
    2. yes: T6 spends them together, so whoever owns one owns the other, and D's owner is the thief;
    3. only if its trail carries ransom money: walk it back hop by hop until it meets the ransom trail or a coin bigger than the trail held;
    4. the chain cannot tell, so call it undetermined.
    Then open that transaction and read its one input's amount. Walk back input by input until you stop: where do you stop, and how much did the first coin hold? Compare it with what the ransom trail held after T4 and with what the victims paid in all. Was your choice right? Write the row.
  5. The agent asks: "So how much of the ransom reached the service?" Choose one and say why: all of T6's single output; 0.10 BTC, both deposits; 0.05 BTC, D alone; or none can be counted, since the sweep mixed it.
  6. Write a short statement for the agent, bottom line first: what reached the service, what the second deposit is, how sure you are, and what the trace does not show.

The problem

Answer the questions above. These hints say where to look.

  1. Amount and block. An address page lists the transactions that pay it and spend from it. The spending transaction is the one that lists the address as an input.
  2. Co-spend. A transaction with several inputs usually means one wallet holds all the keys [4]. The known false positive is a CoinJoin, where several people put inputs into one transaction [9]; day 7 teaches how to spot one. Here, ask whether the outputs look alike.
  3. Payment or change. Analysts look for change by its clues: a new address, the same script type as the inputs, an amount that is not round, the output's position [4] [5]. Check which clues tell the two outputs apart here. Each clue is a wallet habit, so none is proof. Several clues from one transaction are one kind of evidence, so they stop at Moderate.
  4. An output spent with another. Spending two inputs in one transaction ties them to one owner [4]. Ask whether the other input comes from the ransom trail.
  5. Peel and change. In a peel chain each hop pays a small amount, the peel, to one address and sends the rest to a new change address, which does it again [4]. Compare the two outputs' types and amounts.
  6. Position. Compare which output is the change in each hop. A wallet can vary it [5].
  7. The pattern. Services run scripts, so their chains are regular [5]. The pattern alone is one kind of evidence. A peel chain is also an ordinary thing: exchange withdrawals make them [4].
  8. No rule decides. Compare the two outputs of T4 on every clue in hint 3. When no clue separates them, write "undetermined": the course allows it. Then see which output is spent next [5]. Ask what that fact does, and does not, show.
  9. The last hop. Check which of T5's outputs is spent. The one that is not spent is a lead you record, not a trail.
  10. A sweep. A transaction with many inputs and one output gathers several addresses into one. Look at the other inputs: how many times did each receive, and in which blocks? The known false positive is a CoinJoin [9]; day 7 teaches how to spot one.
  11. A batch. A service often pays many customers in one transaction, one output each, from a single input [10]. Peel chains have two outputs. Ask who else the funds went to, and whether you could name any of them. Naming a service from the chain alone is attribution, later in day 6.
  12. Backward. When forward stops at two look-alike outputs, start where the money ended and work back [5]. Each step back is a spend the chain records. Ask which steps are the record and which are still your guess.
  13. Value conservation. A transaction makes no bitcoin: its outputs and fee add up to its inputs, and each input names the earlier output it spends [3]. So a trail can carry no more of the ransom than the ransom addresses received, and a coin bigger than what the trail held cannot be the trail. Habits do not settle it: a change clue is a wallet habit, exchange withdrawals make peel chains too [4], and services' scripted chains are regular [5], so a habit points to a kind of wallet, not one person. Joining trails by habit is the false link that merges two actors: false change links once merged Mt. Gox, Instawallet, BitPay and Silk Road into one cluster of 1.6 million keys [4]. The explorer marks neither deposit as ransom. On a transaction's page each input shows its amount and links to the transaction that made it.
  14. A sweep's total. T6 gathers twelve deposits into one output. Before you count any of it as the ransom's, ask whose money each deposit is. Ask, too, what the chain can say about the service's payouts after the sweep.
  15. The statement. Bottom line first, in plain English, with a confidence word. Say plainly what the chain does not show.

Challenge

Optional, for early finishers. Parts d and e each have a flag: an address or an amount you find on the chain. Type a flag in its box to check it. The page holds only a fingerprint of each flag, not the flag, and nothing is sent or graded. Your instructor has the flags.

  1. T4 has an output that was never spent. Give two explanations. What would you do to tell them apart, and what would change your confidence?
  2. In T7, which output is the change? Which clues point to it and which are no help, given how many outputs the transaction has?
  3. In question 6, the position of the change varied. In the whole trail, which clues were steady and which were not?
  4. Flag: an address. Follow X0, the peel from T1, into the transaction that spends it together with Y. Which output is the payment and which is the change back to X0's owner? Give the payment's address. Does that payment belong on the ransom trail?
  5. Flag: an amount. In the same transaction, how much BTC went back to X0's owner as change? Which clue points to it, and how sure are you?
