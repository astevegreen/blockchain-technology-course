Day 6 lab: follow the ransom to the first service

The course runs a small Bitcoin chain of its own, built for this lab, with the same address formats and transaction rules as Bitcoin. On it, two ransomware victims have paid a ransom (the incident is invented). You follow the money in mempool, an open-source block explorer [1], until it reaches the first service, and write an annotated trace: one row per judgment, each with its heuristic, what could make it wrong, and a confidence.

Write your trace as short notes. Your instructor checks it with you.

This chain is not Bitcoin's. The names and amounts are made up (a course faucet pays every payer and customer, so where the coins first came from is not a question here), and nothing here points to a real person or service. If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages: they show the same transactions, so every question has the same answer there.

Where to start

  • Your instructor tells you where to start: the worked example, or straight at the problem.
  • You may always do the worked example first. It shows you the explorer and one row of a trace.
  • Then the questions: part 1 (questions 1 to 4), part 2 (questions 5 to 9) and part 3 (questions 10 to 12).
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

Reading this explorer

  • This explorer is offline, so its dollar figures, fee badges and "Accelerate" buttons mean nothing here. Ignore them.
  • The lab's blocks were made seconds apart. Use block numbers, not times, to put events in order.
  • The explorer shows no names, and it does not mark which output is change or a payment. Deciding that is your job.
  • An address page lists the transactions that pay it and spend from it. An output with no spend after it has not been spent yet.
  • On a transaction's page, the "Details" button shows each input's address type and the other fields.
  • Address types are visible in the address: bc1q is P2WPKH, bc1p is Taproot (P2TR) and 3 is P2SH [6] [7] [8].
  • Use a window at least 800 pixels wide. A narrower one shows a different, mobile page.

The questions you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: an address, an amount, a block. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [2]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction. Never give a percentage.

Part 1: the ransom addresses

The victims paid two ransom addresses. Victim 1 paid {{addr.R1}}. Victim 2 paid {{addr.R2}}. Call the transaction that spends them T1, the next one down the trail T2, and so on.

  1. How much did each ransom address receive, and in which block? Which transaction spends them, and in which block?
  2. What does T1 tell you about the two ransom addresses? Write the row: what you see, the heuristic, what could make it wrong, and your confidence.
  3. T1 has two outputs. Which is the payment and which is the change? Name each clue you used, and whether it points one way or is no help. Give your confidence.
  4. One output of T1 is spent in block {{tx.tx.height}}, together with a second input. What does that spend tell you about the owner of that output? Do you follow it as the ransom's trail? Say why.

Part 2: the peel chain

  1. Follow the change output of T1 into T2. Which output is the peel (the small amount paid away) and which is the change that rolls on? Which clues tell them apart? Give your confidence.
  2. Do the same for T3, the next hop. The change sat in a different output position in T2 and in T3. What does that say about using position as a clue?
  3. T1, T2 and T3 now form a pattern. Name it and its heuristic, say what could make it wrong, and give a confidence. Record each peel as a lead: its amount, its address, its hop number, and what that address does next.
  4. The next hop (T4) has two outputs. Which clues separate them? Write what you can say about the change, and how you decide which output to follow. Give your confidence.
  5. T5 is the last hop of the chain. Which output is the peel and which is the change? Where does the peel go next, and what is the address's next transaction?

Part 3: the first service

  1. Open the transaction that spends the peel from T5; call it T6. How many inputs and outputs does it have? What does the co-spend heuristic tell you about the peel's address and the other inputs? What could make it wrong, and how would you check? Give your confidence.
  2. T6's single output is spent in one transaction, T7. How many inputs and outputs does T7 have? What does its shape suggest about the wallet that holds the funds? Give your confidence, and say why the trace stops here.
  3. Write one or two sentences an agent could read: where the ransom went, how sure you are, and what your trace does not show.

Worked example: the first victim's payment

This follows the ransom into the first ransom address, a transaction before your trail starts. It shows you the explorer and one finished row. Question 1 onward use the same pages.

Find it

  1. Open the explorer, paste {{addr.R1}} into the search box and press Enter. Its page lists two transactions.
  2. Open the older one, in block {{tx.v1.height}}. Its ID is {{tx.v1.txid}}.

  • Outputs, not balances. Bitcoin records outputs, each an amount locked to an address, and a transaction spends whole outputs [3]. The input here was one {{tx.v1.in.0.btc}} BTC output. {{tx.v1.by.R1.btc}} BTC went to the ransom address; the rest, less the fee, went back to the payer.
  • The fee. No output records the fee: it is what the inputs hold beyond the outputs, inputs minus outputs leaves {{tx.v1.fee}} sats [3].
  • Which output is the ransom? The ransom output is a round number, 1.2 BTC; the other is not. Both addresses are the same type. One clue, so the judgment is below.

The problem

Answer the questions above. These hints say where to look.

  1. Amount and block. An address page lists the transactions that pay it and spend from it. The spending transaction is the one that lists the address as an input.
  2. Co-spend. A transaction with several inputs usually means one wallet holds all the keys [4]. The known false positive is a CoinJoin, where several people put inputs into one transaction [9]; day 7 teaches how to spot one. Here, ask whether the outputs look alike.
  3. Payment or change. Analysts look for change by its clues: a new address, the same script type as the inputs, an amount that is not round, the output's position [4] [5]. Check which clues tell the two outputs apart here. Each clue is a wallet habit, so none is proof. Several clues from one transaction are one kind of evidence, so they stop at Moderate.
  4. An output spent with another. Spending two inputs in one transaction ties them to one owner [4]. Ask whether the other input comes from the ransom trail.
  5. Peel and change. In a peel chain each hop pays a small amount, the peel, to one address and sends the rest to a new change address, which does it again [4]. Compare the two outputs' types and amounts.
  6. Position. Compare which output is the change in each hop. A wallet can vary it [5].
  7. The pattern. Services run scripts, so their chains are regular [5]. The pattern alone is one kind of evidence. A peel chain is also an ordinary thing: exchange withdrawals make them [4].
  8. No rule decides. Compare the two outputs of T4 on every clue in hint 3. When no clue separates them, write "undetermined": the course allows it. Then see which output is spent next [5]. Ask what that fact does, and does not, show.
  9. The last hop. Check which of T5's outputs is spent. The one that is not spent is a lead you record, not a trail.
  10. A sweep. A transaction with many inputs and one output gathers several addresses into one. Look at the other inputs: how many times did each receive, and in which blocks? The known false positive is a CoinJoin [9]; day 7 teaches how to spot one.
  11. A batch. A service often pays many customers in one transaction, one output each, from a single input [10]. Peel chains have two outputs. Ask who else the funds went to, and whether you could name any of them. Naming a service from the chain alone is attribution, later in day 6.
  12. The sentence. Bottom line first, in plain English, with a confidence word. Say plainly what the chain does not show.

Challenge

Optional.

  1. Start at the peel you found in question 9 and walk backward, input by input, to the ransom addresses. At which hops did you need a change guess? Which part of the trail stands on the chain's record alone, and what does that still not show about who controls it?
  2. T4 has an output that was never spent. Give two explanations. What would you do to tell them apart, and what would change your confidence?
  3. In T7, which output is the change? Which clues point to it and which are no help, given how many outputs the transaction has?
  4. In question 6, the position of the change varied. In the whole trail, which clues were steady and which were not?
