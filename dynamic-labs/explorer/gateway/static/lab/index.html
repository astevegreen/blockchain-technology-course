Day 1 lab: read a chain in an explorer

The course runs a small chain of its own: ten blocks of history, built for this lab, that work the way Ethereum's do. You read it in Otterscan, an open-source block explorer [1]. Then you sign a transaction yourself and watch the node add it to a block.

Write your answers down as short notes. Your instructor checks them with you.

If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages. They show the same history, so every question below has the same answer there. For part 2, the saved pages hold a recorded run of the signing page.

Where to start

  • Your instructor tells you where to start: the worked example, or straight at the problem.
  • You may always do the worked example first.
  • After the problem, part 2: you sign a transaction and send it to the node.
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

The questions you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: a hash, a time, an amount. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [13]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction.

  1. Block 5's parent hash is the hash of which block? What would happen to that link if someone changed a transaction in the earlier block?
  2. How many seconds apart are blocks 4 and 5? What decides when this chain makes a block?
  3. Is 0x5FbDB2315678afecb367f032d93F642f64180aa3 an account or a contract? Who created it, and in which block? Give your confidence and the evidence for it.
  4. In block 4, Account 0 sends tokens. Who receives them, how many, and how much ETH does the transaction carry? Why is the receiver not in the transaction's To field?
  5. In block 5, Account 1 sends 2 ETH to a contract. Where does the ETH end up? Give your confidence.
  6. Block 6 holds a transaction that failed. What did it try to do, why did it fail, and what did it still cost its sender?
  7. Block 7 holds two transactions. Who sent each one, and which came first in the block?
  8. How many transactions has Account 3 sent? Which field on its page says so?
  9. The worked example's transaction is in block 3. How many confirmations did it have when block 10 was made? Why does the number in the live explorer keep rising? On Ethereum, would that many confirmations make it final?
  10. The saved pages call 0x9965507D1a55bcC2695C58ba16FB37d819B0A4dc "class drop box". What does the chain itself record about who controls that address? How confident can you be in the label?

Worked example: one transfer in block 3

This walks through one ordinary transfer, field by field. Question 1 onward use the same pages.

Find it

  1. Open the explorer.
  2. Type 3 in the search box and press Enter. You are on block 3's page.
  3. Note the block's timestamp: 14 Sep 2026, 14:00:36 UTC.
  4. Open the block's one transaction. Its hash is 0xaeff71f0c8f69a5dc00df35f2f754423568847cb550ad40121d6d13324aaa779.

Read it

From / Nonce0x70997970C51812dc3A010C7d01b50e0d17dc79C8 (Account 1), nonce 0
Interacted With (To)0x3C44CdDdB6a900fa2b585dd299e03d12FA4293BC (Account 2)
Value1.5 ETH
Transaction Fee0.000016180715292 ETH
Gas used21,000
  • Ledger. Ethereum keeps a balance and a nonce for every account, the way a bank keeps a balance for each customer [5]. Bitcoin, later in the course, works differently: it records unspent outputs and has no account balances [14]. Here, 1.5 ETH left Account 1's balance and joined Account 2's.
  • Nonce. The nonce counts the transactions an account has sent [5]. Nonce 0 means this was Account 1's first. Once a transaction with a given nonce is in a block, the node refuses any other from that account with the same nonce, so the same signed transaction cannot be run twice [5] [8].
  • Fee. The fee is the gas used times the price paid per unit of gas [6]. An ETH transfer to an ordinary account uses 21,000 gas [6] (as of 31 Oct 2026); a call to a contract uses more. The price is the block's base fee, which is destroyed, plus a tip to whoever makes the block [7]. Block 3's base fee was 0.770510251 Gwei, and the tip was 1 wei per gas.
  • From. A signed transaction holds no From field. The node recovers the sender's public key from the signature, and the address is the last 20 bytes of that key's Keccak-256 hash [8] [9]. So the explorer's From is a fact the signature proves, not a claim the sender made.
  • Block. Block 3's page lists its parent hash, which is block 2's hash. Each block names the one before it this way [3].

A statement from this page: "Account 1 paid 1.5 ETH to Account 2 on 14 Sep 2026 at 14:00:36 UTC." This is a fact the chain records, not a judgment: the signature proves Account 1 sent it, and the balances before and after block 3 show the 1.5 ETH moving. A judgment would be a claim the chain does not prove, such as who holds Account 1's key: that needs evidence from outside the chain.

The problem

Answer the questions above. These hints say where to look.

  1. Blocks 4 and 5. Search for 5 and compare its parent hash with block 4's hash. A block's hash covers everything in it [3].
  2. Time. Compare the timestamps on the two block pages. Ethereum divides time into 12-second slots, and in each slot one validator proposes a block [3] (as of 31 Oct 2026). This course chain has a single node that seals a block on a timer instead.
  3. Account or contract. Search for the address. A contract account holds code; an ordinary account is controlled by a private key and holds none [5] [12]. Look for the code (the Contract tab), and for the transaction that created it ("Contract creator").
  4. Tokens. Open block 4's transaction. A token transfer is a call to the token's contract, which records the move in a Transfer log [10]. Read "Tokens Transferred" and the Logs tab as well as "Interacted With (To)".
  5. Internal transfers. Open block 5's transaction. A contract can pass ETH on as part of the same transaction. Those moves are not transactions of their own: the explorer finds them by asking the node to replay the transaction [2]. Look for them on the transaction's page, under Value. The explorer's first TRANSFER line is the transaction's own 2 ETH; the lines after it are what the contract passed on. The saved pages list only those.
  6. A failed transaction. Open block 6's transaction. A failed transaction is still recorded in its block, still pays for the gas it used and still uses up its sender's nonce [6] [8]. Read its Status line, which gives the error.
  7. Two in one block. Open block 7's transactions and open each one. "Block / Position" gives its place in the block: position 0 ran first. The block's list shows the newest first, so do not go by the list's order.
  8. Account 3. Search for 0x90F79bf6EB2c4f870365E785982E1f101E93b906. Its page lists the transactions that touch it. Count the ones it sent and compare with "Transactions sent", which is its nonce.
  9. Confirmations. A transaction's confirmations are the blocks from its own block to the newest, counting its own. The explorer shows them beside "Block / Position"; the saved pages count up to block 10. The live chain keeps making blocks. On Ethereum, a block is final once two-thirds of staked ETH has voted for a later checkpoint, about 15 minutes after it is made [15] (as of 23 Oct 2026); a count of confirmations does not make it final.
  10. Labels. The chain records addresses, never names. A label on an explorer or on these saved pages is someone's note about an address.

Part 2: sign and send a transaction

You need the live chain and a seat number from your instructor. If the chain is down, answer from the recorded run instead.

  1. Open the signing page and load your seat.
  2. Build a transaction of 0.01 ETH to Account 5, sign it, and send it.
  3. Watch the status line: your transaction waits in the node's pool of pending transactions until the next block includes it [4].
  4. Open your transaction in the explorer from the link the page gives you.
  5. Read the panel at the bottom of the signing page: it lists every question the page asked the node [11].

Answer:

  1. Your transaction's hash, its block, and about how long it waited.
  2. Which question did the page ask the node to learn your next nonce? Which one sent the signed transaction?
  3. The transaction you built had no From. What does the explorer show as From, and how sure are you that your seat sent it?

Challenge

Optional.

  1. Account 2 held about 102.1 ETH at block 10. Using its page, list what came in and what went out. Its page shows the block-5 row at the whole 2 ETH the transaction carried: open the transaction to see how much reached Account 2. What is left unexplained, and where must it have come from?
