Day 7 lab: where the trace stops

The course runs a small Bitcoin chain of its own, built for this lab, with the same address formats and transaction rules as Bitcoin. On it, a ransomware victim has paid a ransom (the incident is invented), and the attacker has sent it through CoinJoins and a mixer. You follow it in mempool, an open-source block explorer [1], until it goes dark, and write a limits-of-tracing note: where the trace stops, why, what still shows, and how sure you are.

Write your note as short rows. Your instructor checks it with you; it is the day's graded point.

This chain is not Bitcoin's. The names and amounts are made up (a course faucet pays every payer and mixer user, so where the coins first came from is not a question here), and nothing here points to a real person or service. The leaks are planted for teaching: real CoinJoin wallets are built to avoid them [4]. If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages: they show the same transactions, so every question has the same answer there.

Where to start

  • Your instructor tells you where to start: the worked example, or straight at the questions.
  • You may always do the worked example first. It shows you a CoinJoin in the explorer and one finished row of a note.
  • Then the questions: part 1 (questions 1 to 4), part 2 (questions 5 to 8) and part 3 (questions 9 to 13). Each question has a hint under it that says where to look.
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

Reading this explorer

  • This explorer is offline, so its dollar figures, fee badges and "Accelerate" buttons mean nothing here. Ignore them.
  • The lab's blocks were made seconds apart. Use block numbers, not times, to put events in order.
  • The explorer shows no names, and it does not tell you who owns an output. Deciding what the chain shows is your job.
  • An address page lists the transactions that pay it and spend from it. An output with no spend after it has not been spent yet.
  • A few transactions carry a short text note in an extra output worth 0 BTC, shown as OP_RETURN. Whoever built the transaction wrote it.
  • On a transaction's page, each input shows the transaction it came from. The "Details" button shows the other fields.
  • Use a window at least 800 pixels wide. A narrower one shows a different, mobile page.

How you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: an address, an amount, a block. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [2]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction. Never give a percentage. "Undetermined" is an answer.

Worked example: a CoinJoin with nothing of the ransom in it

This is an earlier round in the same pool, before the ransom was paid. It shows you what a CoinJoin looks like in the explorer, and one finished row.

Find it

  1. Open the explorer, paste {{tx.j0.txid}} into the search box and press Enter.
  2. It is in block {{tx.j0.height}}.

Read it

Inputs{{tx.j0.n_in}}, each {{tx.j0.in.0.btc}} BTC, each from a different earlier transaction
Outputs{{tx.j0.n_out}}, each {{tx.j0.out.0.btc}} BTC, every one a new address of the same type
Fee{{tx.j0.fee}} sats: {{calc.j0_fee_per_input}} sats for each input
  • Equal outputs. Every output is the same amount, so the amount cannot say which input became which output. That is the mark of a CoinJoin: one transaction that several users build together, each signing for their own input [3].
  • The pool. Whirlpool, the CoinJoin design this chain imitates, ran rounds of five inputs and five outputs, later up to eight by eight, all of one pool size such as 0.5 BTC [4]. Each input here carries a little over 0.5 BTC to pay its share of the fee.
  • Do not cluster. The co-spend rule says inputs spent together share an owner. In a CoinJoin they need not: a round of five can hold up to five owners, and the rule would wrongly merge them into one [4].

No confidence word on the stop: it is a fact about the transaction's shape, not a judgment about an owner. The leads you find after a stop each get their own confidence. Questions 3 and 4 ask the same of J1 and J2.

The questions

Part 1: into the mix

The victim paid {{tx.v1.by.R1.btc}} BTC to the ransom address {{addr.R1}} in block {{tx.v1.height}}. Call the transaction that spends that payment T1.

  1. Which block is T1 in, and what are its outputs? Which outputs look alike, and which one does not?

    Outputs. On the ransom address's page, the transaction that lists the address as an input is the one that spends it. Compare the outputs' amounts with the pool sizes in the worked example.

  2. Why would the ransom's owner make outputs of exactly that size? Write T1 as the first row of your note. What could make your reading wrong?

    Pre-mix. Whirlpool first splits a coin into pool-sized outputs in one transaction, before any round [4]. That transaction spends from the address you were tracing. The split is a behavior: ask what else it could be, then what the next transaction (question 3) adds.

  3. Follow two of T1's look-alike outputs into the transaction that spends them; call it J1. Follow the other two into J2. What makes J1 and J2 CoinJoins? What does that do to the co-spend rule for their inputs?

    A CoinJoin. Compare the inputs' sources and the outputs' amounts with the worked example. A CoinJoin is the known false positive of the co-spend rule [5].

  4. How many outputs could each ransom coin have become in J1 (the anonymity set)? How many of J1's outputs belong to the ransom's owner, and can you say which? Answer the same for J2, then write J1 and J2 as rows.

    Anonymity set. The set is the equal outputs a coin could have become. The larger the set, the weaker any guess [6].

Part 2: what leaks

  1. T1's odd output is the toxic change. Find the transaction that spends it; call it M1. What does M1 spend with it? What does that tell you about J1 and J2? Write the row: what could make it wrong, and your confidence.

    After the mix. Outputs spent together after the mix are co-spent like any other inputs [6]. Check that the spending transaction is not itself a CoinJoin: look at its outputs.

  2. Go back to the ransom address's page. What else has happened there since T1? Which CoinJoin output does it tie to the ransom, and by what habit? Write the row with a confidence.

    Reuse. Paying a mixed coin to an address already seen "directly reveals ownership by the same entity"; CoinJoin wallets avoid it [4].

  3. The ransom's owner put two coins into J1. You have tied one J1 output to them. What can you say about the second?

    The second coin. Check every J1 output: where it went next, and whether anything ties it to the ransom. If nothing does, the answer is "undetermined".

  4. The leaks in questions 5 and 6 were planted in this lab. For each, which habit of a CoinJoin wallet would have prevented it?

    Careful wallets. Each leak is a user's habit, not a flaw in the CoinJoin [4] [6]. Ask what the user did with coins from different places.

Part 3: where it goes dark

  1. One of M1's outputs goes into another CoinJoin; call it J3. How many inputs and outputs does it have, and at what amount? Write the row. Does anything in J3 itself pick out the ransom's output?

    A bigger round. Count the inputs and outputs, and compare the amounts with the earlier rounds. Then check whether any leak from part 2 touches J3's outputs.

  2. Look at when J3's outputs are spent. A colleague points at the one that moved last and says "that's the ransom". What do you say, and how sure are you? Write it as a row.

    Timing. Knowing how long a wallet stays in the mix can raise the odds that the outputs leaving at the end are its own [4]. Timing alone is behavior: the confidence scale says how far it goes.

  3. The coin that came back to the ransom address moves on. Follow it until it is spent with other people's coins. A shared label list (invented and unverified) tags {{addr.PW}} as a mixer. What does the chain show about this address, and what does the label add? The mixer later pays out a batch. Which withdrawals fit the coin that went in, and can you pick one? Write the rows.

    A mixer. A mixer accepts coins and sends coins on "in a manner designed to prevent others from tracing" them [7]: the coins out are not the coins in. Look at how the address you followed is spent, and with what. Matching amounts and timing are the habits that can pair a deposit with a withdrawal, each Low alone [8]. A label is one unverified source.

  4. Now work the other way. Take either withdrawal that fits the deposit and walk it backward: open the transaction that paid it, then the transaction that paid that one's input, until you reach the deposits. Where does the walk end? Does it pick out which deposit paid the withdrawal? Write the row.

    Backward. On a transaction's page, each input shows the transaction it came from: follow the inputs back one step at a time. Then try the same walk from a withdrawal that does not fit, and compare where the two walks end.

  5. Write your limits-of-tracing note: one row per step from the ransom address to each place the trace stops, with what still shows after each stop and a confidence for each lead. Then write one sentence that states what your note shows: where the trace stops, why, and what is still worth asking about.

    The note. End each branch at its stop, or at the first service. List each lead with what it is and its confidence. Bottom line first, in plain English, with a confidence word; say plainly what the chain does not show.

Challenge

Optional, for early finishers. Parts a to c each have a flag: an address or an amount you find on the chain. Type a flag in its box to check it. The page holds only a fingerprint of each flag, not the flag, and nothing is sent or graded. Your instructor has the flags.

  1. Flag: an amount. Part of the ransom never entered a second round and has not moved since. How much is it, and at which address? How sure are you that it is the ransom owner's, and why?
  2. Flag: an address. Follow J3's last mover to the address it paid. Name two innocent reasons an output leaves a CoinJoin late.
  3. Flag: an amount. In the mixer's batch, find the withdrawal that is the deposit less exactly 1%, rounded down to the thousand sats. Does knowing that make it the ransom's? Say what else a mixer's fee could be.
  4. Total the BTC that went into the mixer's pool, from every deposit. What share of it came from the address you followed? What does that say about the matching-amount lead?
