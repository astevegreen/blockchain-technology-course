Day 2 lab: a wallet on the course chain

The course runs a small Bitcoin chain of its own, built for this lab, with the same address formats as Bitcoin. You restore a wallet from its seed phrase on the wallet page, then find its addresses in mempool, an open-source block explorer [1]. In part 2 you read a payment that two of three people signed.

Write your answers down as short notes. Your instructor checks them with you.

The seed phrases below are published test vectors. They guard nothing and work only on this training chain. Never type a real seed phrase into a web page. If the explorer or the chain is down, use the saved pages: they show the same transactions, so every question has the same answer there. The wallet page needs no chain.

Where to start

  • Everyone starts with the worked example. It shows you both pages.
  • Then part 1 (questions 1 to 5) and part 2 (questions 6 to 12).
  • The challenge at the end is optional.

Reading this explorer

  • The explorer shows times in your computer's local time. Write them down in UTC.
  • This explorer is offline, so its dollar figures, fee badges and "Accelerate" buttons mean nothing here. Ignore them.
  • The lab's blocks were made seconds apart. Use block numbers, not times, to put events in order.
  • The explorer shows no names, and it does not mark which output is change. Deciding that is your job.
  • On a transaction's page, the "Details" button shows each input's nSequence and witness.
  • Use a window at least 800 pixels wide. A narrower one shows a different, mobile page.

The questions you answer

Some answers are facts the chain records: an address, an amount, a block. Others are judgments about what the facts mean. Give each judgment a confidence word [17]:

HighTwo or more independent lines of evidence agree, and no known failure of the reasoning applies.
ModerateOne strong line of evidence, or weaker lines that agree, but a known false positive could apply.
LowTiming, amount or behavior alone, or one unverified label such as an explorer tag.

Independent means a different kind of evidence, not a second look at the same transaction.

Worked example: Dana's first address

This restores Dana's wallet and follows her first address onto the chain. Question 1 onward use the same pages.

Find it

  1. Open the wallet page. Paste Dana's phrase into section 1, leave the passphrase empty, and press "Derive the wallet".
  2. The first receiving address, index 0, is bc1qcr8te4kr609gcawutmrza0j4xv80jy8z306fyu. This is the address the BIP 84 standard lists for this phrase as a test vector [5], so the page derives the same address as any standard wallet.
  3. Open the explorer, paste the address into the search box and press Enter. Its page lists two transactions.
  4. Open the older one, in block 13 (31 Oct 2026, 18:04:09 UTC). Its ID is f1716cc102e3a41b93b23527a1a23b8ebfd527fcf238c8e1451d5ab2a4193371.

Read it

Inputbc1qkmyuwjwpxcqp82hfcqmphfvzyf7q4acajmthfw, 50 BTC, type P2WPKH
Output 0bc1qlxhay94m94y9jx0meq64e75ld6ahj6tn88kj09, 49.94999436 BTC
Output 1bc1qcr8te4kr609gcawutmrza0j4xv80jy8z306fyu (Dana's receiving 0), 0.05 BTC
Fee564 sats, for a transaction of 141 vB: 4 sats per vB
Input's nSequence (Details)0xfffffffd
  • Outputs, not balances. Bitcoin records outputs, each an amount locked to an address, and a transaction spends whole outputs [12]. The input here was a 50 BTC output. 0.05 BTC went to Dana; the rest, less the fee, went to a new address, which is likely the sender's change.
  • The fee. No output records the fee: it is what the inputs hold beyond the outputs, 50 − 49.94999436 − 0.05 = 0.00000564 BTC [12].
  • The witness. The input's witness has two items, a 71-byte signature and a 33-byte public key. That is how a P2WPKH output is spent: the key must hash to the address, and the signature must match the key [7].
  • nSequence. 0xfffffffd is below 0xfffffffe, so the sender said the transaction could be replaced by one paying a higher fee while it waited [16]. Once it is in a block, that no longer matters.
  • The address. A bc1q address with 42 characters holds a 20-byte hash of a public key [6] [7]. Nothing in it says which wallet or seed it came from.

Back on the address page, the newer transaction (block 16) spends this 0.05 BTC. A fact from these pages: "Address bc1qcr8te…306fyu received 0.05 BTC in block 13 and spent it in block 16." That this address is Dana's is not on the chain: you know it because her phrase derives it. Without the phrase, an analyst would need other evidence to say who controls it.

The problem

Answer the questions above. These hints say where to look.

  1. The path. The wallet page lists each address with its path. BIP 44 names the five levels after m: purpose, coin type, account, change and address index [4]. BIP 84 uses purpose 84 for native SegWit addresses [5]. A level written with ' is hardened [3].
  2. Used addresses. Search for each address from the wallet page. An address with no transactions shows none; the saved pages say "No transactions on this chain touch that address". A restoring wallet looks for used addresses down the list, and stops after 20 unused ones in a row: the gap limit [4].
  3. Payment or change. Open the block 16 transaction. Analysts look for change by its clues: a new address, the same script type as the inputs, an amount that is not round [13] [14]. Check which clues tell the two outputs apart here. Then look for each output address among Dana's receiving and change addresses on the wallet page.
  4. One owner? Spending two inputs in one transaction usually means one wallet holds both keys [13]. A CoinJoin, where several people put inputs into one transaction, is the known false positive [18]. Day 6 comes back to this.
  5. The xpub. An extended public key derives every public key, and so every address, below it on its branch, but no private key [3]. A hardened child cannot be derived from its parent's public key [3]. Compare Dana's account path in section 1 with the treasury path in section 2.
  6. The witness. Open the block 18 transaction and press "Details". A P2WSH output is spent with a witness whose last item is the script itself [7]; the explorer decodes it as "P2WSH witness script". OP_PUSHNUM_2, three keys, OP_PUSHNUM_3, OP_CHECKMULTISIG reads "2 of these 3". OP_CHECKMULTISIG takes one item more than it needs, and that item must be empty [8].
  7. The key order. In section 2 of the wallet page, paste the three phrases and press "Combine the keys" with index 0. Compare each key's receiving public key with the script. BIP 67 sorts the keys before they go into the script [9], and the descriptor's sortedmulti asks for that [10].
  8. Who signed. The two signatures must appear in the same order as their keys in the script [12], but the explorer does not say which key each one matches. Ask whether that order rules out any pair of keys.
  9. Treasury change. Compare each output's type with the input's. Change often goes back to an output of the same multi-signature type [14]. The wallet page's change address (chain 1) at index 0 settles it.
  10. An unspent P2WSH output. Search for the change address. A P2WSH output holds only a 32-byte hash of its script [7]; like P2SH, the script appears only when the output is spent [15].
  11. The sentence. Say what the witness shows, and say plainly what it cannot show. Use the words "multi-sig" and "witness".
  12. A link? Follow the money into each wallet: who paid Dana's addresses, and who paid the treasury? Then compare the master key fingerprint in section 1 with key A's fingerprint in section 2 [3]. The fingerprints appear in the descriptor [11]; ask whether they appear in any transaction.

Challenge

Optional.

  1. Dana also received a payment at receiving address 23. Find it with the wallet page's "Show 20 more", and look it up. Would a wallet restored from Dana's phrase show this payment? Why not, and what would an analyst holding a recovered phrase need to do?
  2. Derive the wallet again with the passphrase TREZOR. What changes? Is the wallet you get "wrong"? [2]
  3. Suppose two of the holders signed their halves of the block 18 transaction separately and combined them [11] [19]. Does anything in the transaction show that two people, rather than one person holding two keys, signed?
